The problem in one minute

A detector is a model that looks at a night-sky image and draws a box around every streak it finds. Each box comes with a confidence, a number from 0 to 1 that says how sure the model is.

The detector in this competition was poisoned. During its training, someone slipped in fake streak patterns and told the model they were real, so it learned to box those too. Teams received the poisoned model, 20 images showing the fake pattern, and 2,000 test images with no answers. The organisers kept one thing hidden: a clean model trained without the poison.

The winning idea

The team stopped trying to build a perfectly repaired model. They asked a narrower question: the poisoned model has already proposed a set of boxes, so how much confidence would the clean model give each one?

That turned the task into adjusting one number per box. Every box in their final file comes from the poisoned model, run with a low bar so that it proposes plenty of candidates. From there the rules were strict:

  • No new boxes are ever added.
  • No box is ever moved or resized.
  • Only the confidence of a box can change, down or up.

They had a reason for the second rule. An earlier experiment that nudged box positions made the public score 9.15 points worse, which suggested the clean model draws its boxes in much the same places as the poisoned one.

The seven stages

Before stage 1

Step 1: train 14 repaired copies

To make a model forget the poison, you show it the poison and tell it to stop. The danger is that it also forgets what a real streak looks like. A model needs examples of what to keep, and the organisers gave none.

So the team built their own. They drew realistic synthetic streaks, brought in labelled photos of real streaks from a public dataset, and made each retrained model copy the teacher on clean parts of the image. Starting from the poisoned model every time, they trained 14 copies using four different recipes.

One disclosure from the write-up: the full-retrain runs also used the unlabelled test images in a limited way. Training matched overall statistics of the model's internal features, averaged over each whole image. No boxes, labels or confidences from test images were used.

Before stage 1

Step 2: collect four kinds of evidence for every box

The 14 models are never merged into one. They are kept apart and treated as a committee. For each candidate box, the team recorded four measurements and saved them.

Stages 1 to 3 · 164.82 → 144.02

Step 3: let the committee vote

The starting file (stage 1) came from seven of the retrained models. For each box it took the most generous opinion among them, so one model that still believed in a box could protect it from the rest. That file scored 164.82.

Then the full committee of 14 went over it. In stage 2, boxes with little support were demoted. This was the biggest single gain of the whole effort, 17.14 points. In stage 3, the veto was widened and a rescue rule was added for boxes that had sunk too low but were still strongly backed.

Stages 4 to 6 · 144.02 → 133.88

Step 4: check the pixels

Voting has a blind spot. If every retrained model is fooled by the same poison pattern, they all vote to keep it. To catch those, the team added evidence that does not come from any model. They measured brightness along each candidate streak and looked for shapes that a real moving object would not produce.

How they kept the tests honest

Every threshold in these tests was fixed in advance using labelled data, before the tests were run on the test images. The public scoreboard was used only to decide whether to keep a finished test, never to tune one. Measurements were also repeated on all eight rotated and flipped views, since a real property of the pixels should not vanish when the image is turned.

Stage 6: two strict demotions

By now most poison was gone, and loosening any earlier rule started to hit genuine streaks. The last two demotions were deliberately narrow. A box was demoted only if it failed a stricter pixel test and the suppression evidence from step 2 was also strong. The gain was small, about 0.12.

Stage 7 · 133.88 → 130.56

Step 5: give confidence back

Every stage so far pushed confidence down. Some genuine boxes ended up lower than they deserved. The scoring rule makes that expensive: on a genuine streak, being under the clean model's confidence costs the full gap, while being over it costs only a tenth of the gap.

Three safety conditions

A box had its confidence restored only if all three held:

  1. All four families of models backed it.
  2. No pixel test had flagged it.
  3. It was never raised above the poisoned model's original confidence.

What it did

On the final run this restored 110 boxes and put back 29.17 units of confidence in total. The score improved by 3.32 points, the largest gain of the closing stretch. No box was added or moved.

After the deadline, and why it worked

One more point they found too late. After the competition closed, the team tried a tip from another competitor's write-up: lowering the confidence given to demoted boxes from 0.01 to 0.00001. With nothing else changed, their file went from 130.56 to 126.16. It does not count toward their result.

The lesson they draw. No single test was a reliable poison detector. The score came from stacking several imperfect checks that fail in different ways: disagreement between models, agreement between families, how far a box was pushed down, stability under rotation, shapes in the pixels, and the lopsided scoring rule. Whenever one check stopped helping, loosening it did damage. Progress came from adding a different kind of evidence.

Can it be reproduced? The team published two notebooks. One retrains everything from the poisoned model and lands at about 131. The other rebuilds the exact winning file, byte for byte, from saved model outputs.
